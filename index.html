AI's Water Bill

How much water does AI actually use, where does it come from, and does your own usage matter? Working conservatively throughout, three answers. A casual user's year of prompting costs less water than one toilet flush, while a heavy agentic user's costs about 160 showers and a fifth of a household's electricity. Roughly five sixths of all of it is consumed at power stations, not at data centers. And nationally it is under one percent of US freshwater consumption, which means the real problem is not scarcity but geography.

Per prompt

Why the answer runs from five drops to a bottle

Every published figure is defensible inside its own boundary. They differ hundredfold because of three choices: whether power-plant water counts, which model and prompt length, and which year's hardware.

Start with the thing everyone argues about. You type a question, a model answers, and somewhere a cooling tower evaporates some water. How much?

The published answers span a hundredfold, and almost all of the spread comes from one decision: whether you count only the water evaporated at the data center, or also the water evaporated at the power station that made its electricity. Those are two separate pipes, and the second one turns out to be four to ten times bigger than the first. The system-level section takes that apart properly. For now it is enough to know that an on-site-only figure and an all-in figure are both honest and are not the same number.

Two settings run through this page. The central case uses Google's measured energy and mid-range water factors. The conservative case uses higher energy, the least efficient major fleet's cooling, and the highest published grid factor; every personal figure here quotes that one. Neither is a best case, and the methods section has the arithmetic.

What moved the number

Energy per prompt fell roughly tenfold between the 2023 literature, which assumed 4 Wh, and 2025 production measurement at 0.24 to 0.34 Wh. Google reports a 33-fold drop in energy per median prompt in the twelve months to May 2025 alone.1 Reasoning and agentic modes pull it back up 3 to 13 times because they emit ten to fifteen times the tokens, and long contexts add more: Epoch puts a 100,000-token input at about 40 Wh.2

Water scales linearly with energy in every published method. So the water question is the energy question multiplied by two factors: the site's cooling efficiency and the grid's water intensity. That is why the electricity section below matters as much as this one.

The bottle, settled

The claim that one prompt drinks a bottle of water comes from a 2023 UC Riverside paper, which actually said one 500 mL bottle per 10 to 50 medium responses, on 2021 hardware that used roughly ten times the energy per answer that measured 2025 systems do. For ordinary chat the viral version is off by a factor of about 260.

But run the conservative numbers on a long reasoning or agentic call and it lands at 56 mL, so nine of those empty a bottle — inside the original paper's range. The famous claim is badly wrong about the typical case and accidentally close on the heavy tail, which is the single most useful thing to know before reading the rest of this page.

Read Priya's row honestly, because it is the one that carries the argument. At the conservative setting her year of agentic coding consumes about 2,700 gallons, which is 160 showers, 44 percent of a year of daily showering, or 9 percent of her household's indoor water. Add amortised training and it is 3,700 gallons. That is not nothing. It is also roughly what four and a half quarter-pound burgers cost in total water footprint, and less than one summer week of watering a small lawn.

Dana's and Marcus's rows carry no argument at all. Dana's entire year of AI is less water than a single flush of an old toilet. Marcus's is under four showers. For anyone whose use looks like theirs, which is most people, the water question is genuinely settled and the honest answer is that it does not register.

Does using less actually do anything?

Not measurably, and this is the one place both sides of the public argument quietly agree. Training runs, provisioned idle capacity and the build-out itself are fixed against any individual's demand; a prompt you do not send does not idle a cooling tower. The levers that do change your own number are model choice, where a reasoning model costs three to thirteen times a standard one per call, and agent discipline, since one delegated task can be fifty model calls. Provider choice matters faintly: AWS and Meta run fleets at 0.12 to 0.2 litres per kWh on-site against Google's 1.15.

Electricity

The electricity is the bigger, more legible story

Water is downstream of energy in every method here, and energy is the thing a person can actually compare to their own life. On that axis the same three users land in very different places.

Table view

The ladder separates the three users far more sharply than the water chart does, because electricity is where the cost originates. Dana's whole year of AI is one kilowatt-hour, less than her phone charger and about thirteen hours of a single LED bulb. Marcus at 43 kWh is a tenth of a refrigerator, or roughly what a laptop draws in ten weeks. Neither is worth a second thought.

Priya at 1,850 kWh is a different kind of number. It is 18 percent of an average US household's annual electricity, 44 percent of one person's share of it, comparable to running a central air conditioner for a season, and more than a clothes dryer and refrigerator combined. With training amortised it is roughly 2,500 kWh, which passes an electric water heater. At 16 cents a kilowatt-hour the electricity behind her agent usage costs somebody about $296 a year, which is a useful reminder that this is a real industrial input and not a free good.

The honest summary is that heavy agentic AI use is an appliance-scale personal energy decision, and light chat use is not a decision at all. Anyone worried about their own footprint should be looking at whether they delegate work to reasoning agents by the hundred, not at whether they asked a chatbot a question this morning.

The conversion that links the two halves of this report

Every kilowatt-hour carries 1.8 to 4.52 litres of water with it, depending on what generated it. That single factor is why the water argument and the clean-generation argument are the same argument. Priya's 1,850 kWh on a coal-and-nuclear grid carries about 2,200 gallons of power-plant water; the same 1,850 kWh from wind carries essentially none. She cannot choose her grid. A data-center developer choosing a site and a power contract can, and that choice moves more water than every cooling-tower upgrade in the industry.

Training

Training is in the national numbers and out of the personal ones

Every per-prompt figure published by a vendor excludes model training. Every national and regional figure includes it automatically. Neither is an error, but mixing them is, and the reason training matters regionally is that it lands on one utility at one time.

Where it is already counted

National and regional totals include training with no adjustment needed. LBNL's 176 TWh and 17.4 billion gallons are measured at the meter and the water main of US data centers. Training runs happen inside those buildings, so their electricity and cooling water are already in the total. The same is true of every local figure in this report: when Google's Council Bluffs campus consumed 1.3 billion gallons in 2025, that number does not care which racks were training and which were serving.

Per-prompt figures exclude it explicitly. Google's paper scopes out "training and model development" by name, as do Microsoft's and Altman's. So does every personal estimate in the next section, unless marked otherwise.

Why training matters regionally even though it is small nationally

Inference load is smooth, distributed and follows users. A training run is a single lump of demand in one building for weeks or months. In July 2022, the month before GPT-4 finished training, Microsoft's West Des Moines cluster drew 11.5 million gallons, about 6 percent of the district's water for that month, from a utility that also supplies household drinking water.27 No annual average would have shown that. It is the clearest illustration in the record that the unit of harm here is a utility in a month, not a country in a year, and it is the reason a state water plan that has no data-center category at all is a real planning failure rather than a rounding error.

Where the water goes

Two pipes, and the second one is bigger

A data center consumes water in two places: its own cooling towers, and the cooling towers at the power plants feeding it. Every study that counts both puts the power-plant share at 83 to 92 percent.

Table view

The gap between those two bars is the largest single disagreement in this literature, and the audit found that it is almost entirely one accounting choice about hydropower. The lower factor counts cooling-tower evaporation at thermal plants, about 0.47 gallons per kWh.8 The higher one is a blended national average that folds in evaporation from hydroelectric reservoirs, charged to power in full. A US grid average built from the 2023 generation mix without hydro computes to roughly 1.0 to 1.25 litres per kWh; adding gross reservoir evaporation at the high end of the literature reproduces about 5; using the net, counterfactual-adjusted hydro figure instead gives about 1.4 to 1.6. The generation section takes this apart, including why the water flowing through a dam's turbines is correctly excluded, why the water evaporating off the reservoir behind it is real but contested, and why NREL itself declined to chart hydropower next to thermal plants. This report uses the higher figure wherever a single value is needed, on the brief to show a reasonable worst case, and labels it as a bound rather than a measurement.

Withdrawal is not consumption. US thermal plants withdraw about 103 billion gallons a day and consume 2.7, because once-through cooling returns the water a few degrees warmer. Evaporative data-center cooling consumes most of what it withdraws. Gallon for gallon, data-center water behaves more like irrigation than like a power plant's intake, which is why "power is 41 percent of withdrawals" is the wrong comparator and why the national share below is computed against consumption.

Which pipe is bigger depends on the cooling

The 83-to-92 percent figure above is a national average, and averages hide the thing that actually decides a given facility's footprint. On-site cooling is the single widest-spread variable in this entire subject — about 220-fold from worst to best — and once you know where a campus sits on that spread, the direct-versus-indirect balance can invert completely.

What the disclosed fleet averages imply

Google reports a fleet water usage effectiveness of 1.15, against 0.27 for Microsoft, 0.19 for Meta and 0.12 for AWS. Since a dry cooler sits near 0.03 and an evaporative tower near 1.8, those averages are only reachable with a particular mix. Solving the blend puts roughly 55 to 63 percent of Google's IT load on evaporative cooling, against something like 4 to 14 percent for the other three.

That is a strategic choice rather than an oversight. Google runs the best power usage effectiveness in the industry at 1.09, and evaporative cooling is how you get there — it is the most energy-efficient way to reject heat. Google traded water for electricity, and its competitors traded the other way. The figures are derived from disclosed averages assuming a two-way mix, so treat them as upper bounds on evaporative share; real fleets include adiabatic hybrids in between.

Three things that fall out of this

The generation-dominates claim is conditional, not universal. For a dry-cooled campus on a thermal grid it is overwhelming, at 99 percent. For an evaporative campus on a clean grid it inverts to 1 percent. The national average lands where it does because most capacity is moderately efficient cooling on a thermal grid, not because the physics forces it.

Cooling choice can outweigh generation choice. An evaporative campus running on renewables consumes about 359 million gallons a year. A dry-cooled campus running on gas combined cycle consumes about 198 million. The "green-powered" facility uses nearly twice the water of the gas-powered one, because it is boiling its own water away on site regardless of what charges the meter.

Dry cooling wins on total water almost unconditionally. It costs about 13 percent more electricity, which on this campus brings back 22 million gallons on gas and 77 million on coal or nuclear — against 348 million saved on site. Break-even would require a grid at roughly 3,100 gallons per MWh, and the worst real thermal plant is about 690. The familiar objection that zero-water cooling just moves the problem upstream is half right: it does move some water, but a small fraction of what it saves.

Why the national number misleads both sides

"Under one percent" is true and beside the point. Water does not move between basins. A 100 MW evaporative-cooled campus draws 1 to 5 million gallons a day from one utility in one watershed, peaking on the hottest days, when that watershed is also under stress. The national total answers "is AI draining America." It does not answer "should Tucson host this."

What the map shows that the statistics do not

The largest existing cluster in the country is in a low-stress basin. Northern Virginia at roughly 4,500 MW is the biggest data-center concentration on earth, and it draws from the Potomac. Atlanta, Columbus and Chicago, the next three, are also in comparatively wet places. The genuinely stressed markets — Phoenix, Silicon Valley, Las Vegas, Salt Lake City — are individually smaller.

The concentration is real, the stress is real, and they only partly overlap. Where they overlap hardest is not the big legacy hubs but the new frontier: West Texas, southern New Mexico, Utah.

The "two-thirds in water-stressed areas" claim, decoded

This is the statistic that carries the entire national argument, and it is narrower than almost every citation of it implies. Bloomberg's 2025 analysis found that roughly two thirds of US data centers built or in development since 2022 sit in water-stressed areas. Three qualifiers matter. It covers new builds only, not the installed fleet. It counts facilities, not megawatts. And it uses basin-level water stress, which measures how heavily a whole river basin is already allocated across farms, cities and industry, not how much any one data center draws.

Treating it as a statement about the existing fleet is a category error, and it happens constantly. The figures below all get quoted in the same conversation as though they were comparable. They measure six different things.

The honest answer to "what share of built data centers are in water-stressed areas"

Nobody knows, and no current figure exists. There is no published, transparent, US-only, capacity-weighted number for the existing fleet. The only peer-reviewed US stock study is Siddik, Shehabi and Marston in 2021, which found about a fifth of direct water footprint and about half of power-linked exposure in stressed watersheds — on 2018 data, before the AI build-out began. The commercial datasets that could answer it properly are proprietary.

What that study did find, and what survives, is sharper than a headline share: western basins host about 20 percent of servers but account for roughly 70 percent of the scarcity-weighted impact. The distribution matters more than the count, which is the same conclusion this page reaches from the local cases.

Where the next five years are going

The hopeful story would be that the industry is migrating to wet regions. That is not what the pipeline shows. Growth is diversifying into the Midwest, Ohio Valley and Gulf Coast while continuing to expand hard in the driest basins in the country. West Texas, sitting on the declining Ogallala and Edwards-Trinity aquifers, is the single largest concentration of new hyperscale capacity in the United States and is projected to be a top-five market by 2028. Utah has one of the ten largest state pipelines despite being among the most water-stressed states. Arizona keeps adding capacity in a metro where some areas can no longer get a potable-water commitment at all.

The constraint is the pipe, not the pond

The most useful recent finding in this literature reframes what "local impact" even means. A 2026 UC Riverside paper argues that the binding constraint on host communities is peak withdrawal capacity, not annual volume. A town's water utility can comfortably supply a data center's average draw on paper and still lack the pipe, pumping and treatment capacity for its hot-day peak — which is exactly when the utility is already stretched.

The scale of that is the striking part. US data centers are projected to need 697 to 1,451 million gallons per day of new water-supply capacity by 2030, against roughly 227 to 604 million if efficiency improves fast. For comparison, New York City's entire average daily supply is about 1,000 million gallons. The infrastructure bill is put at $10 to $58 billion.35

This is capacity, not consumption, and the distinction matters: a facility drawing 5 million gallons on a hot day and 1 million on an average one forces the utility to build for 5. It explains why West Des Moines showed up as a single peak month rather than an annual average, why so many fights are about hot-weather guarantees, and why "their annual use is only X percent of ours" keeps failing to reassure anyone locally. It is also why peak-capacity pressure pushes operators toward dry cooling, which then adds summer electricity demand at the same moment the grid peaks.

One genuinely reassuring counter-current: the largest projects in stressed basins are increasingly being engineered to use almost no water. Abilene's Stargate campus runs closed-loop with dry coolers at roughly 10 million gallons a year. Microsoft is piloting zero-water designs in Phoenix and Wisconsin. Chandler's air-cooled CyrusOne campus uses 180,000 gallons a year across eight buildings. Sitting in a stressed basin and consuming a lot of water are different claims, and the stress statistic cannot tell them apart. What no public dataset yet provides is the share of US capacity actually running evaporative cooling, which is the number that would settle the argument.

Feasibility

Could this be built somewhere with water?

Yes, and the proof already exists. The best cooling performance in the industry comes from cold, wet places that use almost no water. Nothing physical pushes data centers toward dry regions. What pushes them is cheap land, fast permitting, mature tax incentives, fifteen years of accumulated fiber and trades, and water priced at a fraction of its worth.

I had the tradeoff slightly wrong earlier on this page and it is worth correcting, because it is the crux of the question. The conflict is not wet versus dry. It is hot-dry versus cool-wet.

Evaporative cooling works by pushing air toward its wet-bulb temperature, which in a hot dry climate sits far below the air temperature. That is why Phoenix can cool efficiently on a 105-degree day, and why it spends water to do it. A cool climate does something better: it lets a facility reject heat to outside air directly for most of the year and skip mechanical refrigeration altogether. Cool and wet is not a compromise between the two. It is simply the best cell in the grid on both axes at once.

Two complications keep this from being a simple "just build it in Ohio" conclusion.

Wet does not automatically mean low-water. Most of a facility's footprint is at the power plant, so a wet-region campus on a coal-heavy grid can out-consume a dry-region campus on renewables with closed-loop cooling. The sites that solve both halves at once are the ones pairing a cool climate with a low-water grid: the Nordics on hydro, Quebec, and hydro-served upstate New York. That is the genuine best case, not "wet" alone.

The Great Lakes are not the obvious answer either. The basin holds a fifth of the world's surface freshwater and the Compact's diversion ban works as designed against large exports. But the live gap is governance of cumulative in-basin and groundwater use below the five-million-gallon-a-day review trigger, which is where dozens of campuses would actually sit. Worth noting that Ohio's marquee cluster at New Albany is not in the Great Lakes basin at all — it drains to the Scioto and the Ohio River, a conflation that appears often in coverage.

A national problem with no national referee

If the harm is local and the capital is mobile, local government is structurally the wrong level to solve it. When Tucson's council unanimously rejected Project Blue in August 2025, the developer moved to Pima County, which approved the same footprint on unincorporated land and closed the land sale that December. Developers now withdraw proposals before a formal vote specifically so that a public rejection cannot be cited by the next county. That is jurisdiction-shopping as a deliberate strategy, not an emergent pattern.

What nobody has done is net it out. Trackers count blocked projects — roughly $130 billion in the first quarter of 2026 alone — but no study establishes whether that capacity is lost or simply relocated. It is the central empirical question in this debate and it is unanswered.

The answer to "is it inherently impossible"

No. It is inherently possible and currently unincentivised. The physics favours cool wet regions, the best-performing facilities in the world are already there, and the cooling technology that removes water from the equation carries a 20 to 40 percent capex premium that pays back in two to four years and is dwarfed by power-price swings. What keeps capital in dry basins is a bundle of policy and path dependence — permitting speed, tax regimes, accumulated ecosystems — sitting on top of a resource that is priced at roughly a ninth of its replacement value. Every one of those is a choice, and none of them is hydrology.

Generation water, audited

Everything in the chart below is consumption, meaning water that evaporates and does not return to the source. That produces one result worth stating plainly because it inverts the usual intuition: a once-through plant consumes less than a cooling-tower plant of the same type. Once-through coal withdraws roughly 36,000 gallons per MWh against a tower plant's 1,000, but it consumes about 250 against the tower's 687, because the heat leaves in a warmer river rather than in evaporated steam. Withdrawal and consumption do not merely differ in size; for this comparison they rank in opposite orders.

Two corrections from the audit are visible here. Solar is a range, not a number. The 26 gallons per MWh in NREL's table is a median drawn from a small literature dominated by arrays that are actually washed, which in practice means desert installations cleaned several times a year. A fixed-tilt farm in Georgia or Ohio is rain-cleaned and may never be washed at all, putting it at or near zero. Using the median as though it described all solar was the same category of error as using a cooling-tower figure for every coal plant.

And hydropower, which was missing entirely, is the largest factor on the grid.

Is hydropower really the thirstiest source? Mostly an accounting artifact

The flow through the turbines is not consumed. That water continues downstream within minutes, which makes it withdrawal, and it is correctly excluded from every figure here.

The reservoir is a different matter, and the evaporation is real. A large impoundment presents an enormous surface to the sun and the water leaving it does leave the basin. But how much to charge each megawatt-hour is a choice, not a measurement, and the published figures span an order of magnitude because of it.

The famous 18,000 gallons per MWh comes from Torcellini's 2003 NREL study, which took the 120 largest US dams, computed gross evaporation from reservoir surface area, and assigned 100 percent of it to electricity. Those authors flagged the problem themselves, writing that most dams serve more than one function with no easy way to disaggregate, and even pre-empting the marginality objection by noting that if the evaporation happens regardless of generation then the hydroelectric water use "would be irrelevant." Macknick's 2012 review lists a median of 4,491 gallons per MWh but rests on only three data points from two studies, and NREL deliberately kept hydropower out of its own headline comparisons for exactly these reasons. Charting it beside coal and gas, as the first version of this page did, is doing the thing the source says not to do.

The most careful US estimate is Grubert's 2016 analysis, which computes both gross and net. Gross, system-wide: 10,038 gallons per MWh. Net, after subtracting what the pre-dam land and river would have evaporated anyway and adjusting for multipurpose reservoirs: 1,585 gallons per MWh. That is still the highest of any generation source, and Grubert says so, but at roughly 2.3 times median coal and 8 times gas combined cycle rather than the fifteen to ninety times the gross figures imply. Bakken's 2017 review finds allocation methods that assign anywhere from zero to 100 percent of evaporation to power, with the more defensible ones clustering at 35 to 55 percent.

And the punchline for this report: Grubert finds Pacific Northwest net hydro consumption is effectively zero. The Columbia system is more than 40 percent of US hydro generation, and it is cool, wet and heavily run-of-river. So a data center on Columbia River power, which is most of the Oregon and Washington fleet, has a near-zero indirect water footprint under the accounting that survives scrutiny, and an enormous one under the convention that does not.

Two corrections this forces on the rest of the page

The Pacific Northwest claim above was backwards. An earlier version of this section said a campus in The Dalles sits on the grid with the highest allocated water intensity in the country, and that both halves of its footprint were extreme. Under Grubert's net accounting the opposite is true: its grid water is close to zero, and the 29 to 40 percent of city water it draws is the entire story. That makes The Dalles a cleaner case, not a murkier one, because the harm is direct municipal draw rather than a contested allocation.

The national-share denominator mixes frameworks. USGS classifies hydroelectricity as an instream use and has excluded it from national withdrawal and consumption totals since 2000. So the 0.72 percent upper bound earlier on this page divides a numerator that includes allocated dam evaporation by a denominator that excludes it. Put hydro on both sides and the figure is about 0.67 percent; take it off both sides and it is 0.24 to 0.32 percent. The honest consistent range is roughly a quarter to two thirds of one percent, and the headline band on this page is the outer edge of that.

Implication for a solar and storage developer

One caveat first, since the hydro finding cuts against the simple version of this pitch: a campus on Columbia River hydro already has a near-zero net indirect water footprint, so the water argument does not sell in the Pacific Northwest. It sells in thermal-grid load pockets, which is where the growth is.

Water is a real and under-used argument for renewables-served data-center load, and the audit strengthens it rather than weakening it. Wind is zero gallons per MWh. Solar is one to five for the rain-cleaned arrays that make up most of the US fleet, and 26 only where panels are actually washed, which is a desert practice and a choice. Against that, gas combined cycle is about 200 and nuclear or coal with cooling towers is 670 to 690. In water-stressed load pockets, Arizona, Texas, the Front Range and the Great Salt Lake basin, a co-located or contracted solar-plus-storage supply is the difference between a campus that says zero-water and one that is. The one caveat worth carrying into a siting conversation is that desert solar is where washing actually happens, so the arrays serving a Phoenix campus are the ones least entitled to the zero-water claim, though at 26 gallons per MWh they still beat gas eightfold. The regulatory drift supports it: Aurora has banned evaporative cooling outright, Texas is funding water infrastructure while its state plan still has no data-center category, and Georgia is trying to ban the non-disclosure agreements that hide the numbers. Water intensity is on its way to becoming a siting and interconnection screen alongside capacity, and the developer who can document it has an argument nobody else in the queue is making.

What actually moves the number

Generation mix, the largest lever and the least discussed, worth a factor of twenty to four hundred. Siting, wet and cool against Phoenix. Cooling choice, now being written into city codes. Model choice, the one lever an individual holds, worth three to thirteen times per call.

What to say when asked

Your own AI use is almost certainly not a water problem: a normal year is a few showers, and only heavy agentic use reaches appliance scale. Data centers as a class are not a national water problem at well under one percent of consumption. Specific data centers in specific dry or small-utility places are a real water problem, made worse by secrecy, and the fix is where they are built, how they are cooled and what powers them. Most of the water is at the power plant, so the water argument is a clean-generation argument wearing a different hat.

How it was scrutinised

Arithmetic was recomputed, not inherited. Every figure that fed another figure was recalculated from its inputs rather than carried over from a brief. That caught a unit error in my own fuel-cost working, an inconsistency where two different cooling efficiencies had been mixed within a single estimate, and it produced a useful cross-check: one published regional water factor reproduces almost exactly from an independent hydropower figure and that state's hydro share, which is weak evidence both are right.

Primary sources were opened where the number was load-bearing. The hydropower pass extracted raw text from the original reports rather than trusting summaries of them, which is how a widely repeated cooling-tower figure turned out to be off by a few percent, and how the scale of the reservoir-evaporation allocation problem became visible.

Agents were checked against each other. One brief returned a coal water figure roughly thirty times higher than the verified one — a withdrawal number presented as consumption — and called hydropower near-zero, contradicting the hydro pass. It was flagged and not used. Two attribution errors surfaced the same way: a widely quoted water-stress statistic belongs to a different firm than everyone credits, and the European reporting regime that gets described as public per-site disclosure publishes only aggregates.

The rendering was inspected, not eyeballed. Every chart was checked programmatically for labels escaping their bounds, broken footnote links, and navigation drifting out of sync with the sections. Several narrow-width clipping bugs were found and fixed this way.

What the reader changed

The most useful scrutiny came from the person commissioning this, not from the process. Four challenges changed published conclusions:

  • "Most solar farms don't wash panels — that seems harsh as a universal figure." Correct. A median drawn from washing-intensive desert arrays had been applied to the whole fleet. It became a range, and the renewables case improved by roughly fivefold.
  • "Hydropower consumes more than any other source? Is that real?" This unravelled the largest error on the page. It is real only under an accounting convention that charges all reservoir evaporation to electricity, which the most careful study reduces sevenfold. It also reversed a regional claim, exposed a mismatched denominator in the national share, and demoted the report's own conservative factor from a measurement to a bound.
  • "Simple-cycle for baseload? That seems inefficient and loud." Also correct, and the economics confirmed it: a fuel penalty of roughly $120m a year for a gigawatt campus. What I had described as permanent islanding is mostly bridge power, and the decisive variable turned out to be something else entirely — whether the on-site plant rejects heat through a tower or an air-cooled condenser, a fiftyfold swing.
  • "Congestion pricing pulls new generation toward the load, doesn't it?" Yes, and it was missing. Since most of the fleet serving 2030 demand does not yet exist, what gets built and where may matter more than which existing units ramp.

Where it remains weak

Three limits are structural rather than fixable with more effort. There is no validation set: roughly a dozen US facilities have publicly known actual water use, most of it extracted through litigation, so every estimate here carries error bars nobody can measure. Two primary sources stayed shut: a key national report and the underlying methodology of the most-quoted water-stress statistic both refused automated access, and are cited through independent secondary write-ups that agree with each other. And the single most decision-relevant number does not exist anywhere: the share of data-centre capacity actually running evaporative cooling. Without it, no one can say how much of the stress-overlap statistic translates into real water consumption.

The two settings

Every figure on this page is computed at one of two settings. Appendix

What a site-specific water score would take

Everything on this page is a national or regional average standing in for something that is irreducibly local. The honest unit of analysis is a single facility on a single basin drawing from a specific grid. Here is what building that would actually require, and where it breaks.

Why the spatial unit is not fixed

The phrase "water-stressed area" hides a problem: the right boundary depends on how the facility gets its power.

A grid-tied data center consumes its cooling water locally but its generation water wherever the serving plants sit — which, for a campus on PJM, means basins from Illinois to New Jersey. Five sixths of its footprint is not near it at all. It is displaced, not merely diffuse. A campus in Santa Clara may have much of its water physically evaporated in Arizona or off the Columbia, because California imports heavily.

An islanded campus with on-site generation collapses that entirely. Every drop, direct and indirect, lands at one coordinate. The site becomes the unit.

So the same question — "is this facility in a water-stressed place?" — has a different geometry depending on a commercial arrangement invisible from the outside. That is the core reason a national statistic cannot settle a local argument.

The shape of the calculation

A defensible score is a scarcity-weighted volume, not a raw one, because a gallon in the Rio Grande is not a gallon in the Ohio. The standard method for that weighting is AWARE, the ISO 14046 water-scarcity characterisation used in life-cycle assessment.

The score, in words

Direct term: IT energy × cooling efficiency (WUE) × the scarcity factor of the basin the site draws from.

Indirect term: for each serving power plant, the energy attributed to this facility × that plant's water intensity × the scarcity factor of that plant's basin. Summed across the fleet, and across hours, because both the mix and the scarcity move.

The attribution rule is what changes with facility type. Islanded: 100 percent to one on-site plant in the site's own basin, and the two terms collapse together. Grid-tied: distributed across the serving fleet's basins, by marginal dispatch rather than average mix.

The three things that actually block it

Disclosure does not exist. Not in the US, not in the EU, not anywhere. The facility layer must always be inferred, which caps the achievable accuracy no matter how good the rest gets.

Nodal dispatch is confidential by design. Every US grid operator withholds unit-level dispatch. That is a policy decision, so no amount of engineering routes around it.

Validation is the quiet killer. Roughly a dozen US facilities have publicly known actual water use, most of it extracted through litigation or permit conditions. You cannot validate a national model against twelve points. Any score built today is an estimate whose error bars are themselves unmeasurable.

The one group that has actually built most of this

Shaolei Ren's group at UC Riverside has published the closest thing to a working foundation, and their intellectual arc is worth following because it is a steady retreat from the number everyone quotes them for.

2023: water is a hidden footprint with the same on-site versus off-site split that carbon accounting took years to sort out, and most public figures report only the on-site slice. That is the paper the "bottle of water" meme came from.

2024: the footprint is unevenly distributed, and the load-balancing machinery hyperscalers already run for cost and latency could be pointed at equity instead. Naive cost-optimised routing produces a worst-site-to-average burden ratio of about 1.7; an equity-aware objective brings it to about 1.3.

2026: volume is the wrong unit entirely. What matters is stress-adjusted water — a gallon drawn from a drought county in a dry month is a categorically different event from a gallon drawn from a wet county in a wet month — and, more sharply, peak capacity against the host utility's infrastructure. Their community-burden index across ten US sites spans 0.2 percent to 134 percent of the host utility's total capacity, three orders of magnitude, which is the most direct quantification anywhere of why this argument is local.

Two of their findings cut against the alarm rather than for it. Their own per-prompt estimate for a current frontier model has come down to roughly 15 mL total and 5 mL on-site, far below the viral figure. And stress-aware scheduling — shifting compute in time and space — cuts stress-adjusted water by up to 25 percent while also cutting carbon, with rainwater harvesting able to offset the entire on-site cooling load in wet regions. Those are large mitigations available without new hardware.

What you can actually pick up and use

Their 2026 dataset covers 4,147 US data center locations, each annotated with county-level monthly water-stress factors and the water intensity of the electricity subregion serving it, plus precipitation and cooling efficiencies. It is a stress-context lookup table, not consumption telemetry: it tells you how much a gallon at that place in that month should count for, not how many gallons the facility actually draws. Combined with their earlier hourly water-efficiency release and an MIT-licensed reproduction pipeline, that is most of a Tier 1 build already sitting in public.35

Two caveats. There is no hosted calculator or API, so this is notebooks and CSVs rather than a drop-in tool. And the stress-weighting idea is not theirs alone — a separate Purdue group published a closely related adjusted-water-impact metric in 2025.

Two gaps that matter specifically for behind-the-fence campuses

Federal cooling-water reporting covers steam plants at or above 100 MW. Simple-cycle turbines and reciprocating engines — exactly what is going in behind the meter — report nothing. And most data-center blowdown goes to municipal sewer under local pretreatment permits, which federal discharge tracking does not capture at all.

So the facilities whose footprint is most geographically concentrated, and therefore where local harm would be sharpest, are the ones the federal data system is blindest to. Published national models, including the baseline this page relies on, exclude behind-the-meter generation entirely.
